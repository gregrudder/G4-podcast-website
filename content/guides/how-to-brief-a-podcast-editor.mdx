A bad brief produces a polite edit of the wrong episode. The editor removes the ums, levels the voices, and leaves in the story you meant to cut, because you never said it was a story you meant to cut.

Write the brief before the session if you can, and update it the same day while you still remember which take was the good one. This applies whether the editor is in-house at a studio or a freelancer you have never met. G4 quotes [podcast and video editing](/podcast-editing) per job. The quote should absorb the points below. If a point is missing, add it before you accept the price.

## Send the raw files and the shape of the episode

The editor needs:

- the recording, with a note of which file is which if there are several
- the running order
- the target length, even if it is a range (“about 35 to 45 minutes”)
- the title, or “title to be decided”
- names, spellings and job titles for anyone who should be identified on screen

If you recorded multi-camera, say whether you want a locked wide shot or cuts between people. Those are different amounts of work. A wide shot with a conversation under it is closer to an audio edit. Cuts, lower thirds and a branded open are a video edit.

## Mark what must stay and what must go

Do this in plain sentences, not a colour code only you understand.

- “Use the second intro. The first one has the wrong guest name.”
- “Cut the story about the hotel. It is not for the public episode.”
- “Keep the sponsor line exactly as spoken. Do not paraphrase it.”
- “The argument from 40 minutes is the clip. Do not soften it.”

If you do not remember timecodes, describe the moment. “After she laughs about the train” is usable. “Make it punchy” is not.

## Retakes

Say which take wins. If you re-did the open three times, “use take 3” saves the editor from guessing, and from choosing the take where someone swore and then apologised. If every take has a problem, say which problem you will live with.

## Music and rights

If you want music, say whether you have a licence for that specific recording. An editor cannot drop in a chart song because it was on your reference playlist. Production music libraries exist for this. If you have no music and no licence, ask for a dry edit. Silence between sections is better than a track you do not have the right to use.

Also say if a guest played something on their phone in the room. That audio may need to come out.

## Loudness

Agree a number before the export, not in a revision. For stereo speech, a figure you will see often is around −16 LUFS integrated, with true peak below −1 dBTP. Apple’s podcast delivery guidance has used −16 LUFS, give or take 1 LU. YouTube and Spotify do not all use the same target, and music is treated differently from speech.

That number is industry practice, not a published G4 house spec. Put the target you want in the brief. If you do not know, ask the editor what they will deliver and write that into the quote so the second version is not a surprise.

## Captions and vertical clips

If the job includes TikTok, Reels or Shorts, say:

- how many clips
- roughly how long each one is
- whether captions are burned into the picture or supplied as a separate file

Burned-in captions are what most people need for social, because the sound is often off. They need a proofread. Names of people and places are where automatic captions fail. Build one round of caption corrections into the quote if the names are unusual.

Clips are not a free extra on a long-form edit unless the quote says they are.

## Revisions

Agree how many rounds are in the price, and what a round is. “Change the music and recut the open” is a round. “Can you just watch it again and see what you think?” is how rounds become unpaid work, on both sides.

Also agree what happens after the included rounds. A fee for further changes is reasonable. It should be written down before the first cut, when everyone is still calm.

G4 does not publish a revision allowance yet. If you are booking an edit with us, the number of rounds will be in the quote. Ask for it if it is missing.

## Delivery

Decide the files before the edit starts.

- Audio: WAV for a master, MP3 for a smaller upload, or both.
- Video: a master file, and a captioned vertical export if you asked for clips.
- Who uploads to YouTube or the podcast host. Many editors deliver files and stop. Uploading, thumbnails and show notes are extra unless they are listed.

Say how you will receive them: a download link is normal. Agree how long the link stays up. File retention is one of the terms G4 confirms per job rather than on a fixed public policy.

## A short brief you can copy

> Show:  
> Publish as: audio / YouTube / both  
> Target length:  
> People and spellings:  
> Use these takes:  
> Must keep:  
> Must cut:  
> Music: none / library track we will supply / please suggest, we will approve  
> Loudness target:  
> Clips: number, length, captions yes/no  
> Revisions in the price:  
> Deliver: file types, and who uploads  

Send that with the raw files. Then stop adding instructions in separate text messages. One page is the brief. A second page the next morning is a new brief, and it may be a new quote.
